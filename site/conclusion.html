Conclusion

Final Comparison and Historical Argument

42Five turning points

Barbary Wars, 1801–1815: American principle was independence and maritime rights; the strategic issue was trade and credibility; U.S. power defended American interests.

King–Crane Commission, 1919: American representatives investigated local wishes; the imperial settlement did not fully implement them.

Iranian coup, 1953: geopolitical fears and Western interests motivated American covert interference in Iranian political leadership.

Suez Crisis, 1956: concern for international credibility and Soviet influence helped lead Washington to oppose European intervention in Egypt.

Gulf War, 1991: national interest and the defense of Kuwaiti state sovereignty aligned in a multinational response to aggression.

43Why this theme is important

Oil, the Cold War, religion, national security, and competing national movements are each important; none alone explains the entire course from early maritime diplomacy to modern interventions. The tension between ideals and strategic interests allows comparison of policymakers' public principles with their actions and consequences. It helps explain why the United States defended certain claims to sovereignty while limiting or complicating others.

44Connected development across 225 years

1776–1919: American independence began as a national right, then self-government became a more explicitly international principle under Wilson.

1919–1945: diplomatic ideals encountered imperial settlements and petroleum's rising strategic value.

1945–1979: Cold War fears made friendly governments and regional alignments decisive; Iran and Suez show contradictory policies under the same presidency.

1979–1991: the Iranian Revolution exposed long-term vulnerabilities of supporting authoritarian allies, while the Gulf War showed that interests and international sovereignty could align.

1991–2001: American power expanded, yet sanctions, unresolved Israeli–Palestinian claims, and terrorist threats revealed its limitations.

45Final conclusion

The history of American relations with the Middle East demonstrates that the United States did not consistently apply one understanding of sovereignty and political independence. Instead, leaders interpreted these principles through changing perceptions of national interest. During the early republic, independence was primarily something the United States defended for itself. World War I expanded American discussions of self-government into international relations. Petroleum and Cold War competition subsequently made Middle Eastern political relationships strategically important. The 1953 Iranian coup demonstrates how those interests encouraged intervention against another country's leadership. The 1956 Suez Crisis and 1991 Gulf War demonstrate that the defense of sovereignty could also serve American objectives.

By 2001, the United States had become a dominant external power in the Middle East, yet its political influence remained constrained by regional conflicts, competing national movements, and the consequences of earlier decisions.
